Executive Summary Link to heading

The law of the United States[1] is best understood as a system of stacked authorities inside two overlapping sovereigns. At the federal level, the Constitution vests legislative power in Congress, executive power in the President, and judicial power in the federal courts. Article VI then makes the Constitution, valid federal statutes, and treaties the “supreme Law of the Land” against contrary state law. That means there is no single undifferentiated pyramid of “all law”; instead, the first question is always which sovereign has authority over the subject, and the second is which source within that sovereign is highest and validly enacted. [2]

Within each sovereign, the ordinary hierarchy is comparatively clear: constitution above statute, statute above regulation and executive/administrative action, and all of those above private arrangements that conflict with mandatory public law. Treaties complicate the picture because their domestic effect depends on whether they are self-executing or implemented by Congress, and because a later-in-time federal statute can prevail domestically over an earlier treaty. Federal common law also complicates the picture because, after Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins, there is no general federal common law, only limited enclaves for uniquely federal interests. [3]

Conflict resolution in American law is therefore less about memorizing one list than about applying a sequence of doctrines. The most important are the Supremacy Clause and preemption for federal-state conflicts, federalism and anti-commandeering limits on federal power over states, choice-of-law for multistate disputes, stare decisis for precedent, the Erie doctrine for federal courts deciding state-law claims, and the now-historical but still important Chevron framework, which the Supreme Court overruled in Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo in 2024. For practitioners, the operational questions are: What tribunal am I in? What source created the rule? Was it validly promulgated? Has it been preempted, displaced, or overridden? What standard of review applies? [4]

Architecture of Legal Authority Link to heading

American law is built on a distinction between source and force. A source becomes legally controlling only if the institution that produced it had constitutional or statutory authority to do so, followed the required procedures, and stayed within the limits of superior law. That is why the same document type can have different legal force depending on circumstances: a federal regulation validly issued under a statute can preempt contrary state law, while an executive order with no constitutional or statutory footing fails; a treaty may operate directly in domestic courts if self-executing, but otherwise may require implementing legislation; and a contract may govern the parties’ relationship until it collides with a nonwaivable statute, regulation, or constitutional rule. [5]

It is practical rather than absolute because several layers are domain-dependent: treaties and statutes are not always ordered until there is an actual conflict; federal common law exists only in limited fields; and local ordinances depend heavily on state home-rule and preemption doctrines. Conflict Resolution Link to heading

When legal materials conflict, American courts rarely “balance” them abstractly. They apply named doctrines that sort conflicts by source, sovereign, forum, and institutional role. The main question is not merely “which rule is newer?” but “which actor had authority to make a controlling rule for this issue, and what does the governing conflict doctrine say about displacement, deference, or precedent?” [21]

The flow below captures the operational sequence. Courts begin with constitutional constraints, then decide whether a federal rule validly displaces state law, whether Erie or choice-of-law principles require importing state law into federal court, whether agency interpretation receives any weight after Loper Bright, and finally whether precedent already answers the question. [22]

Erie doctrineThere is no general federal common law in diversity cases; federal courts apply state substantive law and federal procedure.Erie Railroad Co. v. Chevron deference and its afterlifeHistorically, Chevron required deference to reasonable agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes. In 2024, Loper Bright overruled Chevron and restored independent judicial judgment under the APA. [28]Courts still respect agency expertise, but they now decide statutory meaning themselves, making source hierarchy and delegation analysis more judicially centered than under Chevron.

Two further conflict rules are especially important. First, treaties and statutes are coequal below the Constitution for domestic-law purposes, so if they truly conflict and the treaty is self-executing, the later-in-time federal rule normally prevails domestically. Second, treaties cannot supersede the Constitution, which is why Reid v. Covert, 354 U.S. 1 (1957), remains foundational. [29]

Branches, Agencies, and Lawmaking Functions Link to heading

The constitutional branches matter because source hierarchy is also a hierarchy of institutional functions. United States Congress[30] makes statutes under Article I, and Congress also creates agencies, defines their jurisdiction, controls appropriations, and may delegate policymaking discretion subject to constitutional limits. The modern nondelegation doctrine remains permissive, but it does not disappear: Congress must still supply an intelligible principle, and recent doctrine shows renewed sensitivity to broad delegations in major policy areas. [31]

The President, under Article II, executes the laws, supervises the executive branch, appoints officers subject to the Appointments Clause, and issues executive orders and proclamations. But presidential instruments are not freestanding lawmaking in the legislative sense: they must rest on constitutional or statutory authority and fail if they contradict a statute or the Constitution, as Youngstown demonstrates. Agencies, in turn, are creatures of statute. Their binding outputs come in two principal forms: regulations under rulemaking authority and orders under adjudicatory authority. [32]

The judiciary’s role is interpretive and remedial rather than legislative. Supreme Court of the United States[33] and the lower federal courts exercise the judicial power vested by Article III, decide cases and controversies, and through adjudication generate precedent that structures later disputes. Marbury anchors judicial review; the APA structures judicial review of agencies; and stare decisis determines how past judicial interpretations constrain future courts. [34]

Procedure, Evidence, and Judicial Review Link to heading

Procedure is not merely “secondary” law. In federal court, it is its own body of enacted law, authorized primarily by the Rules Enabling Act, 28 U.S.C. § 2072, under which the Supreme Court prescribes general rules of practice, procedure, and evidence for the federal courts. The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, Appellate Procedure, Criminal Procedure, and Evidence therefore have binding legal force, though they may not abridge, enlarge, or modify substantive rights and must yield to contrary constitutional or statutory commands. [39]

That is why procedural hierarchy often looks different from substantive hierarchy. Under Erie, federal courts hearing state-law claims do not apply a free-floating federal general common law; they apply state substantive law and federal procedure. As a result, many litigation fights are not about whether state or federal law is “higher” in the abstract, but whether a disputed rule is properly characterized as substantive or procedural. The Federal Judicial Center’s treatment of both Erie and the Federal Rules of Evidence reflects exactly this divide. [40]

Judicial review of administrative action is primarily governed by APA chapter 7. Sections 701–706 identify when review is available and what courts do with final agency action. Under 5 U.S.C. § 706, courts must compel unlawfully withheld action and set aside agency action that is unconstitutional, beyond statutory authority, procedurally defective, arbitrary and capricious, unsupported by substantial evidence where required, or unwarranted by the facts in formal adjudication. Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Association v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co., 463 U.S. 29 (1983), remains the leading federal articulation of arbitrary-and-capricious review. [41]

Administrative adjudication itself is procedural law with substantive consequences. APA § 554 governs formal adjudications, § 556 governs hearing powers and evidence, and § 557 governs initial decisions and agency review. This means that many “agency law” disputes involve several stacked sources at once: the Constitution, the organic statute, the APA, the agency’s own regulations, and the final order under review. [42]

State law is not monolithic. The common pattern is familiar—state constitution above statute above regulation—but the texture of legal authority differs substantially from state to state. Primary Source Gateways Link to heading

The most reliable starting points are official or near-official repositories containing the text of the Constitution, statutes, regulations, and opinions. For federal work, the most useful division is: Constitution and constitutional explanation at Congress, statutes at the U.S. Code, regulations in the Federal Register/CFR ecosystem, and opinions in authenticated U.S. Reports or official Supreme Court PDFs. For state work, the best starting place is usually each state’s own legislative or administrative portal. [55]

Unsettled Areas and Practical Implications Link to heading

The most significant unsettled area in federal public law is the post-Chevron landscape. Loper Bright did not make agencies unimportant; it changed who has the last interpretive word. Courts now exercise independent judgment on statutory meaning, while Kisor’s narrowed deference to agency interpretations of agency regulations remains in place. The resulting litigation environment is still stabilizing, especially where broad delegations, technical regulation, and the major-questions doctrine overlap. [64]

A second unsettled area is the boundary between permissible delegation, major questions, and nondelegation. Congress still delegates extensively, and the formal nondelegation doctrine remains comparatively forgiving. But recent doctrine and commentary within official constitutional materials show ongoing skepticism toward broad delegations touching major economic and political questions. For practitioners, that means delegation challenges are no longer fringe arguments in administrative law. [65]

A third unsettled area is the constitutional and practical line between treaties, executive agreements, implementation statutes, and termination authority. Official constitutional materials emphasize that self-execution remains contested in application, and that the Constitution does not clearly specify how treaty termination authority is allocated. In domestic litigation, this means lawyers must not assume that an international commitment automatically creates a judicially enforceable federal rule. [66]

A fourth unsettled area lies in state-local preemption and home rule. The four representative states all authorize meaningful local self-government, but they do so in different constitutional and statutory forms. As a result, the relative force of a local ordinance against state statutes, statewide policy, or state constitutional provisions can vary dramatically even when the subject matter looks similar. For practitioners, this is the main reason that “state law” cannot be treated as interchangeable across jurisdictions. [67]

The practical lesson is straightforward. A competent hierarchy analysis usually proceeds in five moves: identify the forum and sovereign; identify the source type; verify the source’s authority, promulgation, and finality; run the appropriate conflict doctrine; and then apply the correct standard of review. Most hard legal research problems in American law are resolved not by finding “more law,” but by locating the right source at the right level, then asking the right conflict question about it. [68]

Open questions remain. This report is representative rather than a 50-state treatise; it does not catalog every state-specific preemption doctrine, every agency-specific rule on precedential administrative decisions, or the full law of executive agreements and tribal law. Those are the main areas where a matter-specific jurisdictional supplement would usually be necessary. [69]
