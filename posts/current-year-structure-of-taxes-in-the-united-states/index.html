Executive summary Link to heading

This report uses the latest official data available as of April 27, 2026, but the data year differs by topic. Current federal rate parameters are for tax year 2026; the latest full-year federal revenue data are FY2025 actual receipts; the latest official all-federal distributional-incidence data are calendar year 2022 from the Congressional Budget Office[1]; the latest IRS-based individual income-tax percentile data are tax year 2023; and the latest national state-local tax data are Q4 2025 annualized BEA data plus rolling four-quarter 2025 Census QTAX data and 2025 annual state tax collections released in April 2026. [2]

At the federal level, the tax system remains dominated by individual income taxes and payroll taxes. In FY2025, individual income taxes generated about $2.684 trillion and payroll taxes about $1.766 trillion, together accounting for roughly 84 percent of federal receipts. Corporate income taxes raised $456 billion; customs duties rose sharply to $194 billion; excise taxes raised $105 billion; and estate and gift taxes raised $31 billion. [3]

For tax year 2026, the Internal Revenue Service[4] reports that the ordinary federal individual income tax keeps its seven-rate structure—10, 12, 22, 24, 32, 35, and 37 percent—with the top rate beginning at $640,600 for single filers and $768,700 for married couples filing jointly. The standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers and $32,200 for joint filers. The federal estate tax basic exclusion amount rises to $15.0 million in 2026. [5]

The most important recent federal legislative change is the One, Big, Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025), which the IRS says significantly changed federal taxes, credits, and deductions and made the 2017-style seven-rate individual schedule effectively permanent. Other recent structural changes still shaping the code are the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, which created the 15 percent corporate alternative minimum tax for very large corporations and funded a large IRS modernization/enforcement effort, and the subsequent rescissions and appropriations laws that reduced part of that funding. [6]

Federal tax expenditures remain enormous. Treasury’s budget estimates for FY2026 indicate that some of the largest revenue losses come from the exclusion of employer-provided health insurance, retirement saving preferences, and preferential treatment of capital gains and dividends. On Treasury’s FY2026 estimates, the employer health exclusion is roughly $296 billion, retirement-savings exclusions are on the order of $156 billion, and lower rates on capital gains and dividends about $135 billion. [7]

Distributionally, the federal system is still progressive overall, but not uniformly so across tax instruments. The latest CBO distributional release shows that in 2022 the effective federal tax rate rose from about 1.4 percent for the bottom quintile to 23.2 percent for the top quintile and 31.5 percent for the top 1 percent. For the narrower individual income tax alone, IRS-based published data for tax year 2023 show the top 1 percent still paid a disproportionately large share of tax relative to income. [8]

Compliance remains a central fiscal issue. The IRS’s latest official tax-gap page reports an average gross tax gap of $496 billion for tax years 2014–2016, with a projected average gross tax gap of $540 billion for 2017–2019, and a projected net tax gap of $470 billion after late payments and enforcement. In FY2024 the IRS closed 505,514 audits producing over $29.0 billion in recommended additional tax. [9]

Nationally, state and local taxes are structurally different from federal taxes because property taxation is central and local governments matter much more. Using the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis[10] current-receipts framework for Q4 2025, state and local current tax receipts were annualized at $2.725 trillion, composed mainly of property taxes ($805 billion), income taxes ($793 billion, personal plus corporate), sales taxes ($633 billion), and inferred excise taxes of roughly $294 billion. The U.S. Census Bureau[11] QTAX release for rolling four-quarter 2025 also shows combined state-local taxes of about $2.211 trillion, with property and sales taxes still the largest categories. [12]

California’s tax system is unusually reliant on the personal income tax. The California Department of Finance[13] says the personal income tax is the state’s largest revenue source and is estimated to account for nearly 60 percent of General Fund revenue before transfers in 2024-25, while the sales tax is about 15 percent. The same forecast emphasizes California’s sensitivity to capital gains and high-income taxpayers, and the Legislative Analyst’s Office[14] continues to stress revenue volatility. [15]

Federal tax architecture in the current year Link to heading

The federal tax system is best understood as a layered system combining broad-based taxes on labor and business income with narrower taxes on capital transfers, specific goods and activities, and imports. In the current-year parameter set, the main federal tax instruments are: individual income tax, corporate income tax, payroll taxes for Social Security and Medicare, preferential capital-gains taxation layered on the individual tax, the estate and gift tax, excise taxes, and customs duties (tariffs). [16]

The current ordinary federal individual income tax keeps the seven-rate structure. For tax year 2026, the IRS states that the rate schedule for single filers is 10 percent up to $12,400, then 12 percent above that, 22 percent above $50,400, 24 percent above $105,700, 32 percent above $201,775, 35 percent above $256,225, and 37 percent above $640,600. For married filing jointly, the corresponding threshold amounts are $24,800, $100,800, $211,400, $403,550, $512,450, and $768,700. The standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers, $32,200 for joint filers, and $24,150 for heads of household. [5]

For capital gains, the structure still differs from ordinary income: short-term gains are taxed at ordinary rates, while long-term gains and qualified dividends continue to face the familiar 0 percent / 15 percent / 20 percent schedule, with a separate 3.8 percent net investment income tax still applying above statutory thresholds. The IRS’s inflation-adjustment revenue procedure left that basic structure intact for 2026; contemporaneous summaries of the 2026 thresholds report the 0 percent bracket extending to roughly $49,450 for single filers and $98,900 for joint filers, with the 20 percent bracket beginning above roughly $545,500 and $613,700, respectively. [17]

The corporate income tax remains centered on the 21 percent statutory federal corporate rate established by the 2017 reform, but it is no longer the whole story. The IRS notes that the Inflation Reduction Act created a 15 percent corporate alternative minimum tax on the adjusted financial statement income of very large corporations, generally those with average annual financial statement income above $1 billion. That means the federal corporate regime is now a combination of the ordinary 21 percent tax, the CAMT backstop, and other targeted provisions such as the stock-buyback excise tax created by the same law. [18]

The payroll-tax structure continues to rely on statutory Social Security and Medicare rates. The Social Security Administration[19] says that for 2026 the OASDI contribution and benefit base is $184,500. The OASDI tax rate is 6.2 percent each for workers and employers, or 12.4 percent for self-employment income. Medicare remains the uncapped companion payroll tax. [20]

The estate and gift tax remains a relatively small but symbolically important part of the federal system. The IRS says that estates of decedents dying in 2026 have a basic exclusion amount of $15.0 million, up from $13.99 million for 2025. [5]

Excise taxes and tariffs do not operate through a single bracket schedule. Excises are product- or activity-specific statutory taxes; tariffs are line-item and country/legal-authority specific charges collected at the border. That distinction matters analytically: unlike the income tax, those taxes change relative prices and can have visible incidence on consumers, importers, exporters, and supply chains rather than simply on taxable cash income. [21]

The tariff regime is especially fluid in 2026. As of April 27, 2026, the official federal position is that a temporary 10 percent ad valorem import surcharge under section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 applies to covered imports beginning February 24, 2026, for a period of 150 days, subject to exclusions and interactions with other tariff authorities. The United States Trade Representative[22] and U.S. Customs and Border Protection[23] also indicate that importers must still navigate the broader product-specific tariff schedule and other trade remedies. [24]

Major recent federal tax-law changes Link to heading

The federal structure in force today reflects several stacked policy changes rather than one clean redesign. The most important are the 2017 corporate-rate cut, the 2022 IRA business taxes and IRS funding, the 2025 OBBB individual-business changes, and the 2026 tariff reset. [26]

Federal revenue, tax expenditures, and distribution Link to heading

The latest full-year federal receipts data show how concentrated the federal system is in a few taxes. In FY2025, total receipts were about $5.291 trillion. Of that, individual income taxes raised $2.684 trillion, payroll taxes $1.766 trillion, corporate income taxes $456 billion, customs duties $194 billion, excise taxes $105 billion, and estate and gift taxes $31 billion. Customs duties were the fastest-growing line item, up $111 billion year over year. [3]

The equally important analytical point is that the federal government also spends heavily through the tax code. Treasury’s tax-expenditure tables show that the largest federal tax expenditures are not niche preferences but broad structural subsidies embedded in the tax base: exclusions for employer-provided health insurance, retirement savings provisions, and lower rates on capital gains and dividends. Those are functionally comparable to very large spending programs delivered through reduced tax liability rather than through annual appropriations. [27]

On distribution, the broad federal system remains progressive, but the story depends on whether one is looking at all federal taxes or at only the individual income tax. CBO’s latest all-federal distribution release covers 2022 and shows a strongly progressive pattern in effective tax rates. Published summaries of that release report effective rates of roughly 1.4 percent for the bottom quintile, 23.2 percent for the top quintile, and 31.5 percent for the top 1 percent. CBO also emphasizes that realized capital gains remain highly concentrated near the top of the distribution. [28]

For individual income tax alone, the latest IRS-based published data are for tax year 2023. Those data show the top 1 percent continued to bear a disproportionately large share of the federal individual income tax: a recent summary of the IRS data reports that the top 1 percent earned 20.6 percent of AGI and paid 40.4 percent of federal individual income taxes in 2023. That is narrower than all-federal incidence, but it is the latest percentile-based federal income-tax snapshot available from IRS data. [29]

Compliance and enforcement Link to heading

The federal revenue system is materially constrained by noncompliance. The IRS’s official tax-gap page reports an average gross tax gap of $496 billion for tax years 2014–2016 and a projected average gross tax gap of $540 billion for 2017–2019. After late payments and enforcement, the projected net tax gap for 2017–2019 is $470 billion. The IRS estimates that about 85.1 percent of taxes are paid voluntarily and on time, rising to about 87 percent after enforcement and late payments are accounted for. [31]

The composition of the tax gap is also telling. For 2017–2019 projections, the IRS attributes the largest component to underreporting ($433 billion), followed by underpayment ($66 billion) and nonfiling ($41 billion). That is analytically important because it suggests the central compliance problem is not simply collections on known liabilities; it is incomplete or inaccurate reporting, especially where third-party information reporting and withholding are weak. [31]

Operationally, the IRS still examines only a small share of returns, but examination rates rise sharply for the highest-income taxpayers. On the most recent out-of-statute year shown in the IRS Data Book tables, the exam coverage rate for individual taxpayers reporting total positive income above $10 million was 11.0 percent; for $5 million to $10 million it was 3.1 percent; and for $1 million to $5 million it was 1.6 percent. In FY2024, the IRS closed 505,514 audits and recommended over $29.0 billion in additional tax. [32]

Enforcement capacity remains in flux because the post-IRA funding path has changed meaningfully. The IRS FY2026 budget request states that it seeks $9.8 billion in annual appropriations, down from the FY2025 enacted level, and also proposes to rescind $16.5 billion in unobligated IRA balances. IRS budget documents also note earlier rescissions under the Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023 and subsequent appropriations laws. The result is that the enforcement and modernization agenda remains operationally active but fiscally less secure than the original 2022 plan envisioned. [33]

State and local taxes nationally Link to heading

State and local taxation is structurally different from federal taxation in three ways. First, property taxes matter enormously and are predominantly local. Second, sales and excise taxes are more central than at the federal level. Third, there is no single national state model: some states rely heavily on income taxes, others on sales taxes, and many localities rely on property taxes. The Census Bureau’s QTAX program explicitly notes that it provides the current national and state-by-state view of these collections, and the 2025 Annual Survey of State Government Tax Collections was released on April 15, 2026. [34]

Using the BEA current-tax-receipts framework for Q4 2025 (annualized), state and local current tax receipts totaled $2.725 trillion. The major building blocks were property taxes ($805.1 billion), income taxes ($606.8 billion personal + $186.3 billion corporate = $793.1 billion), sales taxes ($633.0 billion), and inferred excise taxes of about $294.5 billion after subtracting sales, property, and “other” taxes from taxes on production and imports. That leaves a smaller residual “other” tax category. [35]

The rolling four-quarter 2025 Census QTAX totals tell a similar story using a different accounting frame: total combined state-local taxes of about $2.211 trillion, made up of $832.5 billion in property taxes, $607.4 billion in general sales and gross receipts taxes, $592.8 billion in individual income taxes, and $178.6 billion in corporate income taxes. That framework is useful because it is directly tied to the Census tax-collections system rather than the national accounts. [36]

Variation across states remains substantial. The legal structures differ on whether a state imposes a broad-based individual income tax at all, whether the income tax is flat or graduated, how much local-option sales taxation is allowed, and how tightly property-tax growth is constrained. The latest official Census release confirms that all 50 states continue to show different mixes across the five broad state-tax categories and detailed subcategories. [37]

Two high-level conclusions follow from the national data. First, property taxes are still the backbone of local finance, making them far more important in the state-local system than in federal finance. Second, the state-local system is generally less progressive than the federal system because sales and excise taxes loom larger and because local property taxation is only partly offset by income-tax progressivity in states that have it. The exact degree of regressivity varies widely by state design. [38]

California deep dive Link to heading

California combines a highly progressive personal income tax with a relatively broad sales tax and a constitutionally constrained property-tax system. The latest detailed official personal income tax schedules available as of April 27, 2026 are for tax year 2025, published by the Franchise Tax Board[39]. Those schedules retain the long-standing progressive rate structure beginning at 1 percent and reaching 13.3 percent at the top end, with detailed thresholds adjusted for inflation. The latest FTB business-rate page shows an 8.84 percent corporation tax rate, 10.84 percent for banks and financials, a 6.65 percent corporate AMT, and a 1.5 percent S-corporation rate. [40]

California’s property-tax structure is defined by Proposition 13. The California State Board of Equalization[41] explains that Proposition 13 rolled assessments back to 1975 values, limited the general property-tax rate to 1 percent of assessed value plus voter-approved debt rates, and limited annual assessed-value growth until reassessment events. That gives California a very different property-tax profile from states that reassess more frequently at market value. [42]

On revenue composition, the California Department of Finance says the personal income tax is estimated to account for nearly 60 percent of General Fund revenue before transfers in 2024-25, and the sales tax about 15 percent. California’s 2026-27 budget forecast also says the personal income tax and corporation tax were the main drivers of the revenue upgrade relative to the 2025 Budget Act forecast, while the sales-tax forecast was revised slightly downward. [43]

That concentration in the PIT is what drives California’s fiscal volatility. The same Department of Finance chapter says the upgraded personal income-tax forecast is tied heavily to capital gains realizations and high-end withholding tied to technology-intensive sectors, while the Legislative Analyst’s Office continues to frame California’s reserve and budget policy around revenue volatility. In plain terms, California’s tax base is productive but unusually exposed to equity markets and a relatively narrow slice of high-income taxpayers. [15]

A particularly important recent California-federal interaction concerns the pass-through entity elective tax (PTET). The 2026-27 Governor’s Budget says that, because federal H.R. 1 / Public Law 119-21 permanently extended the federal SALT cap, California’s earlier statutory trigger now extends the state PTET from 2026 through 2030. Relative to the 2025 Budget Act forecast, that extension is projected to increase corporation tax revenue by $22.9 billion and reduce personal income-tax revenue by $20.2 billion over the budget window, with a small positive net timing effect. That is an important example of state tax design responding directly to a federal deduction cap. [44]

California also uses the tax code as a major spending instrument. The 2025-26 California tax-expenditure report shows very large PIT and corporate tax expenditures. On the PIT side, among the largest estimated 2025-26 revenue losses are the exclusion of employer pension contributions ($17.0 billion), exclusion of employer contributions to health plans ($10.0 billion), exclusion of Social Security benefits ($5.5 billion), basis step-up on inherited property ($4.9 billion), exclusion of capital gains on sale of a principal residence ($4.4 billion), charitable-contribution deduction ($3.9 billion), and home-mortgage-interest deduction ($3.0 billion). Corporate-side standouts include the water’s-edge election ($4.5 billion) and the research and development credit ($1.5 billion) in 2025-26. [45]

Refundable and targeted credits are smaller in fiscal size but important distributionally. California’s tax-expenditure report lists the Earned Income Tax Credit at roughly $950 million in 2025-26 and the Young Child Tax Credit at $430 million. The FTB says that for tax year 2025 the CalEITC can be worth up to $3,756 for eligible working families and individuals earning up to $32,900. The state also provides a renter’s credit and a foster-youth credit, which the tax-expenditure report quantifies at much smaller aggregate amounts. [46]

Open questions and limitations Link to heading

Two limits matter for interpretation. First, the latest official all-federal distributional-incidence data are 2022, so any statement about current-year burden by decile/percentile requires either interpolation or model-based estimates; this report therefore relies on CBO for 2022 and IRS-based percentile data for the narrower 2023 individual income tax. Second, California had not yet published a full tax-year 2026 PIT schedule as of April 27, 2026, so the California rate discussion uses the latest official detailed schedules then available, which were for tax year 2025. [50]
